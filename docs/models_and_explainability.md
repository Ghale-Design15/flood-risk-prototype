# Models and Explainability

*Report section — Ghale*

## 1. What the models predict

Every model answers the same question: **will tomorrow be a high river level day at Murray
Bridge?** A day counts as high risk when the water level reaches the 0.80 quantile of the
historical record, which is 0.806 m. About 20% of days meet that condition, so the problem
is imbalanced and accuracy alone would be misleading.

All models share one training base (`notebooks/common.py`) so the comparison is fair. They
use the same four features derived only from past days, which avoids leakage:

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `level_lag1` | water level 1 day ago |
| `level_lag2` | water level 2 days ago |
| `level_roll7` | mean level over the 7 previous days |
| `level_change3` | level 1 day ago minus the level 4 days ago (the trend) |

The data is split **chronologically**, training on the earlier period and testing on the
most recent 954 days. An earlier shuffled split produced an inflated Random Forest F1 of
0.932; with lagged features a random split lets the model see days that sit between two
training days, so that result was leakage and was discarded.

## 2. The models

- **Logistic Regression** — a linear baseline. Standardised inputs, balanced class weights.
- **Random Forest** — 400 trees, depth capped at 10. The depth was chosen with an
  expanding-window backtest inside the training period only; fully grown trees fit the past
  better but generalise worse to a later period.
- **XGBoost** — gradient boosting, tuned with Optuna.
- **LSTM** — a sequence model that reads the last 14 daily levels rather than the four
  summary features. Its decision threshold (0.72) was selected on a validation slice.
- **Ensemble (deployed default)** — an equal-weight soft vote that averages the predicted
  probabilities of the three tabular models.

## 3. Results

Chronological test fold, 954 days, positive class = high risk day.

| Model | F1 | MCC | RMSE | Brier | NSE |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Persistence baseline | 0.796 | 0.732 | – | – | – |
| Logistic Regression | 0.800 | 0.741 | 0.300 | 0.090 | 0.505 |
| Random Forest | 0.799 | 0.734 | 0.264 | **0.070** | **0.618** |
| XGBoost | 0.802 | 0.738 | 0.276 | 0.076 | 0.582 |
| LSTM | 0.804 | 0.741 | 0.338 | 0.114 | 0.371 |
| **Ensemble (deployed)** | **0.806** | **0.743** | 0.271 | 0.074 | 0.596 |

The tabular rows come from `docs/metrics.json` (regenerated by `notebooks/eval_metrics.py`).
The LSTM row comes from its own notebook: it needs a 14-day window, so it drops slightly
more early rows and its test fold is 953 days against 954. The two are comparable in period
and size but are not scored on a byte-identical fold, which is worth stating rather than
hiding.

**The honest headline is that every model sits within about one F1 point of the persistence
baseline.** The baseline simply predicts that tomorrow repeats today, and it scores 0.796.
River level is highly autocorrelated, so yesterday's level is already an excellent
predictor and the machine learning models add a small margin rather than a dramatic one.
Reporting this openly matters more than presenting a flattering number.

Within that narrow band the ordering is still meaningful:

- The **ensemble** gives the best F1 and MCC, which is why it is served as the default. Its
  soft vote smooths the individual models' errors.
- **Random Forest** gives the best probability quality (lowest Brier, highest NSE). This is
  relevant because the dashboard shows a probability, not just a yes or no.
- The **LSTM** matches the tabular models on F1 and MCC, but its probabilities are the least
  calibrated (Brier 0.114). It learns the sequence shape well and expresses confidence less
  reliably.

**Metric choice.** F1 balances precision and recall on the minority (flood) class. MCC is
reported because it accounts for all four cells of the confusion matrix and is not inflated
by the majority class. RMSE, Brier and NSE assess the probability itself rather than the
hard 0/1 call, which is what the operator actually reads on screen.

## 4. Explainability

Two complementary methods are used, chosen to suit each model.

### Signed contributions for the Logistic Regression

Because the model is linear, each feature's contribution to a single prediction is exact:
the coefficient multiplied by the feature's distance from a typical day. The dashboard
shows these as signed bars, so an operator can see which conditions pushed risk up and
which pulled it down. The model is a pipeline containing a scaler, so the current point and
the reference point are both passed through the scaler before the difference is taken;
otherwise the contributions would be misstated by the scaling factor.

### SHAP for the tree models and the LSTM

SHAP attributes a prediction to its inputs with a consistent, additive game-theoretic basis.
Two explainers are used, matched to the model type:

- **TreeExplainer** for Random Forest and XGBoost (exact and fast for tree ensembles).
- **GradientExplainer** for the LSTM (gradient based, appropriate for a neural network),
  with a background sample drawn from the training period.

SHAP values are **precomputed** in `notebooks/precompute_shap.py` and saved as `.npy` files
in `shap/`. The dashboard loads those files instead of recomputing on every page load,
because SHAP over a test fold is far too slow for an interactive screen.

**What SHAP shows (mean absolute impact, share of total):**

| Feature | Random Forest | XGBoost |
|---|---|---|
| `level_lag1` (yesterday) | 53% | 58% |
| `level_roll7` (7-day mean) | 30% | 26% |
| `level_lag2` (2 days ago) | 10% | 10% |
| `level_change3` (trend) | 6% | 6% |

Both tree models agree: **yesterday's level dominates, and the 7-day average is second.**
Together they account for over 80% of the attribution. This is physically sensible for a
large regulated river, and it also explains the earlier result: since the models lean so
heavily on yesterday's level, they cannot pull far ahead of a baseline that *is* yesterday's
level.

For the **LSTM**, SHAP is computed per day of the 14-day input window. Importance rises
steadily toward the present: days -1, -2 and -3 carry about 47% of the total attribution
between them, while days -10 to -14 contribute very little. The model learned to weight
recent history most heavily without being told to, which is a useful independent
confirmation of the tree models' behaviour.

### Why explainability matters here

The system proposes flood alerts to a human operator who must authorise them. A probability
alone is not enough to justify that decision. Showing which conditions drove the score lets
the operator sanity check the model against what they can see in the catchment, and the
authorisation is recorded in a tamper-evident audit log. The model advises; a person
decides.

## 5. Limitations

- Only Murray Bridge has a trained model. Other council areas on the map are context.
- The improvement over the persistence baseline is small, as reported above.
- Feature importance is global, so it describes the model overall rather than the specific
  day on screen. Per-prediction SHAP would need live computation, which was traded away for
  a responsive interface.
- The LSTM's probabilities are poorly calibrated and would need calibration before any
  operational use.
